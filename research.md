Pakistani E-Commerce Consumer Vernacular: Linguistic Corpus and Sentiment Analysis of Daraz.pk Reviews across 33 Product Categories
===================================================================================================================================

The rapid digitisation of retail in South Asia has produced a distinctive digital sociolect characterized by heavy lexical borrowing, morphological blending, and non-standardized phonetic orthography. Within Pakistan’s primary domestic e-commerce marketplace, Daraz.pk, customer feedback diverges sharply from standard literary Urdu and formal South Asian English. Instead, consumers employ Roman Urdu—Urdu transcribed phonetically into the Latin alphabet—interspersed with functional English loanwords, vernacular idioms, and unpunctuated run-on syntactic streams.

A systematic corpus analysis across white goods, kitchen appliances, personal grooming devices, and home utilities reveals structural linguistic conventions. Orthographic variance is driven by mobile-keyboard layout constraints, rapid phonetic transcription, and consonant cluster reductions (e.g., _boht_ versus _bht_ versus _bohot_; _hai_ versus _hy_ versus _h_). Furthermore, rating distributions (5-star, 4-star, and 3-star) reflect distinct discursive patterns: 5-star ratings emphasize relational relief (_same as shown_), operational reliability, and domestic harmony; 4-star ratings confirm core utility while noting secondary logistical or physical defects (e.g., short cables, shipping delays, minor scuffs); and 3-star ratings convey pragmatic resignation, where device performance meets minimum functional tolerance but is undermined by perceived material cheapness or unmet performance expectations.

*   Common closing phrases: _10/10_, _recommended_, _paisa wasool item_.
    

Linguistic Insights and Cross-Category Patterns
-----------------------------------------------

The compiled corpus across these thirty-three retail divisions highlights consistent communicative dynamics that define Pakistani e-commerce discourse. These linguistic structures operate across morpho-phonology, syntactic code-mixing, structural punctuation, and socio-cultural framing.

### Orthographic Instability and Consonant Compression

Because Roman Urdu functions without standardized orthographic governance or institutional style guides, word boundaries and character spellings remain fluid. Typographical variants are largely shaped by mobile QWERTY keyboards, quick typing, and phonetic spelling.

*   Auxiliary verb constructions undergo regular vowel reduction: the present auxiliary _hai_ alternates between _hy_ and bare consonant _h_. In 4-star and 3-star reviews where typing pace reflects frustration, the ultra-abbreviated _h_ dominates.
    
*   Intensifiers display similar lexical compression: _bohot_ and _boht_ compress to the consonant cluster _bht_.
    
*   Voiced labiodental and retroflex dental variations emerge in orthography: _thora_ (a little) appears as _thra_ or _tora_; _theek_ (fine/correct) oscillates between _thek_, _thk_, and _tik_.
    
*   Pluralization markers frequently drop terminal nasalization: reviewers write _deewaro_ instead of literary Urdu _deewaron_ (walls), _garmiyo_ instead of _garmiyon_ (summers), and _kapro_ instead of _kapron_ (clothes).

### Code-Mixing and Syntactic Integration

The reviews demonstrate intra-sentential code-mixing adhering to a matrix language frame where Urdu supplies the grammatical framework (postpositions, verb morphology, auxiliary verbs) while English supplies nouns, descriptive adjectives, and technological predicates.

Reviewers rarely translate domain-specific functional concepts into Urdu. They incorporate direct English loanwords (_suction power_, _touch buttons_, _crema_, _auto shut off_, _non stick coating_, _tip-over switch_, _viewing angle_). However, the inflectional morphology remains strictly Urdu: _vibrate krti ha_, _chilled kar deta ha_, _burn ho jata hy_. When negative attributes are described, English nouns are paired with Urdu light verbs (_leakage ka dar rehta hy_, _damage receive hua_).

### Structural Punctuation Habits and Syntactic Rhythms

Authentic Pakistani e-commerce reviews feature minimal formal punctuation. Standard terminal full stops, semicolons, and commas are rarely used. Instead, text forms a continuous stream-of-consciousness run-on sentence.

Clauses are conjoined via coordinating and adversative conjunctions (_par_, _lekin_, _lkn_, _bs_, _but_, _to_, _wrna_). Reviewers rely on the terminal auxiliary markers (_h_, _hy_, _tha_, _thi_) to signal the completion of an idea before immediately transitioning to the next observation. Ratings of 5 stars rely on trailing religious or emotional honorifics (_MashaAllah_, _Alhamdulillah_, _JazakAllah_, _shukriya seller_) to close the thought, whereas 3-star reviews often finish abruptly with pragmatic evaluative summaries (_guzara hy_, _normal chez hy bus_, _average performance_).

### Socio-Cultural Contextual Markers

A primary discriminator between authentic Pakistani consumer reviews and programmatic or bot-generated text is the presence of domestic, familial, and infrastructural context. Reviewers frequently ground their satisfaction in family approval:

*   Items are explicitly situated as gifts for female family heads (_ammi k liye lia tha_, _ammi boht khush hoi_), dowry preparations (_dahez k liye pack rkha hy_), or child-rearing aids (_bacho k lunch k liye jaldi roti ban jati h_).
    
*   Regional urban realities feature prominently in evaluations. Heating and cooking appliances are reviewed against municipal gas outages (_gas load shedding ka behtareen tod hy_); air purifiers are judged by their performance against the winter particulate crisis in northern Punjab (_Lahore ki smog k liye lifesaver hy_); and cooling appliances are assessed by their ability to run on off-grid battery systems (_ups pe b asani se chal jata hy_).
